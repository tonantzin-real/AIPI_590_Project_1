The problem

When an algorithm rejects someone, today's explanations (e.g. SHAP) say why: "your payment history raised your risk." That helps the lender, but not the applicant, who needs to know what to do next.

Audience: people applying for credit, especially first-time and underserved borrowers, not the lender.

Research questions

RQ1
How actionable are SHAP's explanations for rejected applicants?
RQ2
Can we offer realistic plans using only changes a person can make today?
RQ3
Is that path to approval equally available across sex, age, education, and marital status?

2What I built

1
Credit model. LightGBM on 30,000 credit card clients (UCI, Taiwan 2005), ROC-AUC 0.78. Rejected if predicted default risk ≥ 50%.
2
Why? (SHAP). Which of each person's top reasons could they actually change?
3
What to do? (DiCE). Plans that only change what a person controls, in plain language.
4
Fairness audit. All 1,749 rejected test applicants, across 4 demographic dimensions.
5
Web app where anyone can try a profile.

Key design decision: what can a plan change?

✓ Can change
Payment status: only improve
Balance: only go down
Payment: only go up, up to the bill
🔒 Locked
Who you are: sex, age, education, marital status
The past: previous months
The lender's: credit limit

Effort = how big a plan's changes are (normalized L1 distance). Group gaps tested with Fisher's exact and Welch's t-tests, corrected for multiple comparisons.

Example: one rejected applicant

24 years old · 2-month delay · $45,000 balance · paid $500 last month

✗ Rejected: 84.5% predicted risk of default

SHAP: why?
  1. Payment status, this month ✓ can change
  2. Bank-set credit limit ✗ can't change
  3. Amount paid, 2 months ago ✗ can't change
Our plan: what to do
  1. Catch up on payments:
    2-month delay ➔ minimum payment made
  2. Pay more this month:
    $500 ➔ $16,281

✓ Approved: risk drops to 46.3%

3What I found

RQ1 · SHAP explains the past, not the next step

68% of SHAP's top reasons are things the applicant can't change today.
49.5%
of rejected applicants: the #1 reason is not actionable
27.7%
get no actionable reason at all in their top 3

RQ2 · Realistic plans are possible for about half

47.2% of rejected applicants (825 of 1,749) get a realistic plan using only 3 things they control.

RQ3 · Equal effort, unequal access

Effort is the same for everyone, but applicants who are 30 and older or married get a plan less often.

Out of the rejected applicants in each group, how many got a plan?

Effort, once a plan exists: about the same for every group (group ratios 0.93x to 1.01x). No difference is significant.

4Takeaways

  1. Explanations should be actionable. Attribution alone leaves most rejected applicants without a path forward.
  2. Audit access, not only effort. Here the unfairness is who gets a plan at all.
  3. Realistic plans are possible for about half of rejected applicants.

Limitations

  • Taiwanese data (2005), not Mexican; amounts are not in pesos.
  • Rejections are simulated from predicted default risk.
  • Only 3 things can change; no income or savings data.
  • Effort is statistical, not the real cost for a given person.
  • Age and marital status overlap; not tested jointly.

Risks & responsible deployment

  • Unaffordable plans could push people to informal loans.
  • Plans are guidance, not guarantees.
  • Explanations need audits to avoid fairwashing.
